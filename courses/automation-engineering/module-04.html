Module 04

Queueing Theory for Job Systems

Will your workflow queue keep up at 10x lead volume, or fall over? This module introduces the M/M/1 queue model and Little's Law, giving you the actual math to answer that question before it happens to a client in production.

Learning objectives
  • Apply Little's Law to relate arrival rate, service rate, and queue length
  • Compute utilization and expected wait time for a single-server job queue
  • Recognize when a system is approaching the utilization cliff where wait times explode
Before you start

What this module assumes

Modules 1–3 covered the shape of workflows, the guarantee retries provide, and how to fail gracefully. This module asks a different kind of question, not "does it work," but "does it keep up." A workflow queue can be perfectly correct and still fall over purely from volume, and that failure mode has its own math, independent of anything you've built so far.

What's new

This is the first module with real arithmetic, not just formal notation. You'll compute actual numbers: utilization, expected wait time, queue length. The payoff is concrete: by the end you'll be able to look at a client's job volume and processing capacity and tell them, with a formula instead of a guess, how bad their wait times will get if volume grows.

What's assumed

Modules 1–3 completed. Basic algebra, fractions, and reading a formula as a recipe.
No statistics or probability background required, the one distributional fact used (exponential service/arrival times) is stated, not assumed.

1

The question that matters: will it keep up?

A client's lead-enrichment worker processes jobs one at a time. It currently handles their volume fine. They're about to run a campaign that will multiply lead volume. Will the queue still drain in a reasonable time, or will it back up indefinitely? "It's fine, we have headroom" is a guess. Queueing theory turns it into a calculation.

Definition: M/M/1 queue

A queueing model with Poisson arrivals (jobs arrive at some average rate λ, independently of each other), exponentially distributed service times (a single server completes jobs at some average rate μ), one server, and unlimited waiting room. The "M/M/1" name is standard notation: Markovian arrivals, Markovian service, 1 server. It's the simplest queueing model with a full closed-form solution, and a good first approximation for a single worker process pulling jobs off a queue.

Definition: utilization

ρ = λ / μ (rho), the fraction of the server's capacity that's occupied on average. If jobs arrive at 8/minute and the server can process 10/minute, ρ = 0.8, the server is busy 80% of the time on average.

The claim this module proves: a queue is only stable, meaning it doesn't grow without bound, if ρ < 1. And as ρ approaches 1, wait time doesn't grow proportionally, it grows explosively. "We're at 80% capacity" and "we're at 95% capacity" sound like similar levels of comfortable, they are not even close.
Practice check

A queue has λ = 7 jobs/min and μ = 10 jobs/min. Without a calculator: is ρ closer to "comfortably under 1" or "dangerously close to 1"? What does that suggest about how much headroom this system has for a volume increase?

Check your answer
ρ = 7/10 = 0.7, comfortably under 1. That suggests real headroom exists, roughly 43% more volume (λ up to just under 10) before the server can't keep up on average at all, though as later sections show, wait times start climbing well before ρ actually reaches 1.

Little's Law

Before the M/M/1-specific formulas, one relationship holds for any stable queueing system, regardless of arrival pattern or service time distribution. It's not an approximation or a special case, it's an accounting identity.

Little's Law

L = λW, where L is the average number of jobs in the system (waiting plus being served), λ is the average arrival rate, and W is the average time a job spends in the system from arrival to completion.

Proof sketch (Little, 1961)
  1. Consider a long time interval of length T. Track the total "job-time" accumulated, the sum, over every job that passed through the system, of how long each one stayed.
  2. That total equals the area under the curve of "number of jobs in the system" plotted over time, which is exactly L × T by definition of the time-average L.
  3. It also equals the number of jobs that arrived during T, approximately λT, multiplied by the average time each one spent, W.
  4. Setting the two expressions for the same quantity equal: L × T = λT × W, and dividing both sides by T gives L = λW. ∎

Nothing here assumed Poisson arrivals, exponential service, or even one server. This is why it's called a law, not a formula specific to M/M/1, it holds for any stable system where jobs enter and eventually leave.

Practice check

Run the simulation twice with the same settings. Are the measured L and W identical both times? Does L ≈ λW still hold both times regardless? What does that tell you about Little's Law versus the specific numbers it relates?

Check your answer
L and W themselves vary between runs, because the simulation is random. But L ≈ λ_realized × W holds up both times regardless of what L and W individually turn out to be. Little's Law isn't a prediction of what L or W will be, it's a constraint relating whatever they turn out to be. That's the difference between a law and a formula for computing a specific value.
3

Why ρ must be less than 1

Little's Law relates L, λ, and W but doesn't tell you what any of them are for a given system, that requires a model. For M/M/1, the derivation starts with a basic requirement: the server has to clear jobs, on average, at least as fast as they arrive.

Claim: a queue with ρ ≥ 1 is unstable, queue length grows without bound

Argument. Think of the queue length as a random walk: each tick, a job arrives with probability related to λ (pushing the walk up) or the server finishes one with probability related to μ (pulling it down). The walk's average drift per tick is proportional to λ − μ. If ρ = λ/μ ≥ 1, then λ ≥ μ, so the drift is zero or positive, there's nothing pulling the walk back down on average. A random walk with zero or positive drift doesn't stay bounded, it wanders arbitrarily high given enough time, exactly like a fair or biased-upward coin-flip walk. Only when λ < μ (drift strictly negative) does the walk have a restoring force strong enough to keep the queue length's long-run average finite. ∎

Why "ρ ≤ 1" isn't good enough: at exactly ρ = 1, the drift is exactly zero, not negative. That's the boundary case where random fluctuations alone, with no restoring force, let the queue wander arbitrarily large. Stability needs strictly less than 1, and as the next section shows, the formulas confirm this isn't just a technicality, wait time literally diverges to infinity as ρ → 1.
Practice check

A queue has ρ = 1.05 (arrivals slightly faster than service). Does that mean the queue is instantly overwhelmed, or does the argument above predict something more gradual?

Check your answer
Gradual. The drift argument says the average trend is upward, not that every tick adds a job with certainty. Random fluctuations mean the queue length can still dip down sometimes, but the persistent positive drift means that, over a long enough time horizon, it trends upward without bound. In practice this looks like a queue that seems "mostly okay" for a while, with occasional bad days, before it visibly and permanently backs up as the drift compounds.
4

Deriving wait time

Before the formula, the intuition it's going to formalize. Picture a single checkout line: a cashier serves customers at some steady rate, and customers arrive at some steady rate. If arrivals are much slower than checkout, the line is almost always empty, a new arrival gets served almost immediately. As arrivals creep up toward the cashier's actual processing rate, the line doesn't grow politely in proportion, it starts to compound: each customer's wait depends on everyone ahead of them also having had to wait, and that dependency chain is what turns "a bit busier" into "the line is now genuinely long." That compounding is exactly what the algebra below makes precise, not a new idea, just the checkout-line intuition turned into a formula you can compute instead of eyeball.

For a stable M/M/1 queue (ρ < 1), the probability of finding exactly n jobs in the system in steady state follows a geometric distribution, which lets you derive the average queue length in closed form.

Derivation: L = ρ / (1 − ρ)
  1. In steady state, the rate of transitions from n−1 jobs to n jobs must equal the rate of transitions back, giving the balance equation P(n) = ρ · P(n−1), so by induction P(n) = ρⁿ · P(0).
  2. Probabilities must sum to 1: P(0) · Σρⁿ = 1. Since Σρⁿ = 1/(1−ρ) for ρ < 1, this gives P(0) = 1 − ρ.
  3. The average number in system is L = Σ n·P(n) = Σ n(1−ρ)ρⁿ. Working through the sum (a standard series identity) gives L = ρ / (1 − ρ). ∎

Applying Little's Law (L = λW) to solve for W: W = L/λ = 1/(μ − λ). Subtracting the actual service time 1/μ gives the average time spent waiting, before service even begins: Wq = λ / (μ(μ − λ)).

The shape that matters: Wq has (μ − λ) in the denominator. As λ approaches μ (that is, as ρ approaches 1), that denominator approaches zero, and Wq shoots toward infinity. This isn't linear growth, it's a hyperbola. Doubling how close you are to the cliff doesn't double the wait, it can multiply it by ten or a hundred.
Practice check

Compute Wq for λ=8, μ=10 (ρ=0.8), then for λ=9.5, μ=10 (ρ=0.95). λ increased by less than 19%. By roughly what factor did Wq increase?

Check your answer
At ρ=0.8: Wq = 8/(10×(10−8)) = 8/20 = 0.4. At ρ=0.95: Wq = 9.5/(10×(10−9.5)) = 9.5/5 = 1.9. That's roughly a 4.75x increase in wait time from under a 19% increase in arrival rate, exactly the nonlinearity the formula predicts. This is the calculation to actually run before telling a client "a bit more volume will be fine."

Practice check

Move the slider from λ=5 to λ=6 (a 20% increase), then from λ=9 to λ=9.7 (an 8% increase). Which of those two moves increases Wq more, in absolute terms? What does that tell you about where small forecasting errors matter most?

Check your answer
The second move, an 8% increase near the cliff, increases Wq far more than the first, a 20% increase in a comfortable zone. A small forecasting error ("we said 9, it turned out to be 9.7") barely matters at low utilization but can be catastrophic near the cliff. This is exactly why you need to know how close to ρ=1 a system already is before dismissing a modest-looking growth forecast.
6

Operating below the cliff

The practical consequence of the hyperbola in Section 4 is a standard piece of capacity-planning guidance: don't run a single-server job queue at steady-state utilization much above 70–80%, even though "80% busy" sounds like it has margin. At that point you're already on the steep part of the curve, and normal day-to-day variance in arrival rate (a busy Monday, a marketing push) can push you further up the curve than the average number suggests.

What actually fixes a queue near the cliff: two levers exist, reduce λ (rate-limit intake, shed or defer lower-priority jobs) or increase μ (faster processing, or add a second server, which changes the model to M/M/c and shifts the cliff further out for the same per-server utilization). Adding capacity is usually the real fix; the math here tells you when you need it, before the wait times make it obvious the hard way.
Practice check

A team says "we'll just add a second worker if the queue gets slow." Based on this module, what's the problem with waiting for the queue to visibly get slow before acting?

Check your answer
Because of the hyperbola, "visibly slow" arrives suddenly and late, not gradually and early. Wait time stays low and unremarkable for a long stretch as ρ climbs, then rises very fast once ρ gets close to 1. By the time it's visibly slow, ρ is likely already deep in the danger zone, and the fix (adding capacity) takes time to deploy, time during which the queue keeps compounding. The math is valuable precisely because it gives you a warning before the symptom does.

Cross-module check

Module 3 covered retries with backoff. If a downstream failure causes a burst of retries to hit your own queue, what happens to λ (arrival rate) in this module's terms, and how does that connect the two modules?

Check your answer
A retry burst is a temporary spike in λ, the same jobs arriving again on top of new ones. If your queue was already running at a moderate ρ, a retry storm can push λ close to or past μ, right into the steep part of the Wq curve from Section 5, exactly when the system is already struggling. This is why Module 3's jitter (spreading retries out over time instead of bunching them) isn't just about being polite to a downstream API, it's also protecting your own queue's utilization from a self-inflicted spike.

Glossary

M/M/1 queue — Poisson arrivals, exponential service, one server, unlimited waiting room.
λ (lambda) — average arrival rate.
μ (mu) — average service rate of one server.
ρ (rho) / utilization — λ/μ, the fraction of server capacity occupied on average; must be < 1 for stability.
Little's Law — L = λW; holds for any stable queueing system regardless of distribution.
L — average number of jobs in the system (waiting + in service).
W / Wq — average time in system / average time spent waiting before service begins.
utilization cliff — the region near ρ=1 where wait time grows hyperbolically rather than linearly.
