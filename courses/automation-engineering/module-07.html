Module 07

Observability & SLOs

You cannot fix what you cannot see, and you cannot promise a client reliability you haven't defined numerically. This module covers SLIs, SLOs, and error budgets as a rigorous framework, plus structured logging and distributed tracing basics for diagnosing a failure across a multi-step pipeline.

Learning objectives
  • Define an SLI/SLO/error-budget triplet for a real client system and justify the numbers
  • Design structured log events that make a specific failure diagnosable in under five minutes
  • Trace a single business event across every step of a multi-stage pipeline
Before you start

What this module assumes

Modules 1–6 gave you the tools to build a workflow that's correct, resilient, appropriately scaled, secure, and contractually sound. None of that tells you whether it's actually working right now, or lets you prove it to a client with a number instead of a feeling. This module closes that gap.

What's new

The diagnostic exercise in Section 6 uses the exact same lead pipeline from Module 1 (Receive → Validate → Enrich → Score → Assign), now under concurrent load with real, interleaved, structured log lines. You'll filter them the way you'd actually have to in production, by a correlation ID, to find one failure buried in the noise of several pipelines running at once.

What's assumed

Modules 1–6 completed, especially Module 1's pipeline example, which this module's diagnostic exercise builds directly on.
Basic percentage arithmetic, no statistics or monitoring-tool experience required.

1

"It's usually fine" is not a reliability promise

Every system has some failure rate. The question a client actually needs answered isn't "does it ever fail," it's "how much failure is acceptable, and how do we know when we've exceeded it." Answering that requires three specific, related definitions, not a vague sense of quality.

Definition: SLI (Service Level Indicator)

A specific, numeric measurement of one aspect of the system's behavior. Example: "the percentage of lead-enrichment requests that complete in under 2 seconds." An SLI is just a measurement, it has no target attached yet.

Definition: SLO (Service Level Objective)

A target threshold for an SLI, over a stated time window. Example: "99.5% of lead-enrichment requests complete in under 2 seconds, measured over a rolling 30 days." The SLO is the promise; the SLI is what you measure to know if you're keeping it.

Definition: error budget

1 − SLO, the amount of "badness" you're explicitly allowed before you've broken the promise. A 99.5% SLO has a 0.5% error budget. This reframes reliability work: the goal isn't "zero failures," it's "stay inside the budget," which is an achievable, measurable target instead of an impossible one.

Why stating this numerically changes behavior: "we're pretty reliable" can't be checked. "99.5% of requests under 2 seconds over 30 days" can be checked automatically, every day, and tells you exactly how much room you have left before you've broken a real promise, not just a vague sense that things feel slow lately.
Practice check

A team says "our SLO is 99.9% uptime." What's missing from that statement, using the definitions above? (There's a real gap, not just a nitpick.)

Check your answer
The time window. 99.9% over what period, a day, a month, a quarter? A single bad hour blows a much bigger hole in a daily budget than a monthly one, and "we're within SLO" is meaningless without knowing the measurement window it's being evaluated against. A complete SLO always states both the target and the window.

Move the slider from 99.0% to 99.9% (one more nine), then from 99.9% to 99.99% (another nine). Both moves add exactly one nine. Does the allowed monthly downtime shrink by the same absolute amount both times?

Check your answer
No, 99.0%→99.9% drops allowed downtime by roughly 6.5 hours, while 99.9%→99.99% drops it by roughly 39 minutes, a much smaller absolute change, even though both are "one more nine." Each additional nine cuts the error budget by the same RATIO (10x smaller), not the same absolute amount, and the ratio compounds, which is exactly why going from three nines to four nines is a much harder engineering problem than going from one nine to two, even though they look like the same size of change on paper.
3

Choosing an SLI and SLO honestly

Two failure modes show up constantly when teams set these numbers. First, picking an SLI that's easy to measure instead of one that reflects what users actually experience, "server process is running" is trivial to measure and tells you almost nothing about whether leads are actually getting processed correctly. Second, picking an SLO that sounds impressive instead of one the architecture can actually support, promising 99.99% on a system with a single-instance database and no failover is a promise you already know you'll break.

The honest process, in order: (1) pick an SLI that reflects real user-facing behavior, not internal plumbing. (2) Look at what the system has actually achieved historically, not what you wish it achieved. (3) Set the SLO slightly below or at that historical baseline, tightening it over time as reliability work (Modules 3 and 4's material, retries, circuit breakers, capacity headroom) actually earns it. An SLO is a promise you intend to keep, not an aspiration.
Practice check

A client wants a 99.99% SLO on lead processing. Their current queue runs at ρ=0.85 utilization (Module 4), with no circuit breaker on their enrichment API dependency (Module 3). Is 99.99% a defensible number to commit to today?

Check your answer
No, not without changes first. ρ=0.85 is already deep in the steep part of the Module 4 utilization curve, meaning wait-time spikes are a real, foreseeable risk, and no circuit breaker means a struggling enrichment API can degrade the whole pipeline instead of failing fast, exactly the Module 3 material. Committing to four nines on this architecture as it stands is committing to a promise the system isn't built to keep. The honest move is to either set a lower SLO that matches current reality, or invest in the capacity and resilience work first and raise the SLO once it's earned.
4

Structured logging

A log line like "enrichment failed" tells you something went wrong and almost nothing else. Which lead? Which attempt? How long did it run before failing? Was this part of a retry sequence? A structured log event answers all of that by design.

Definition: structured log event

A log entry as a consistent, machine-parseable record (typically JSON) with a fixed set of fields, rather than free-form text. At minimum: a timestamp, an event name, a correlation ID (also called a trace ID) tying it to one specific business event, the relevant entity IDs, an outcome, a duration, and error detail if applicable.

The single most valuable field: the correlation ID. Everything else describes one moment; the correlation ID is what lets you reconstruct an entire journey across multiple steps and services, by filtering for every log line that shares it. Without it, under any real concurrent load, you cannot reliably tell which "enrichment failed" line belongs to which "lead received" line from ten seconds earlier, timestamps alone don't disambiguate overlapping concurrent events.
Practice check

Two leads are being processed at nearly the same moment. Both produce a log line "validation passed" within the same second. Without a correlation ID, what's the risk in assuming you know which line belongs to which lead based on timestamp order alone?

Check your answer
Under concurrent execution, processing order and log-emission order aren't guaranteed to match arrival order, one lead's steps can interleave with another's, or a scheduler/queue can reorder work. Assuming timestamp order equals identity is exactly the kind of assumption that works fine in casual testing (where things rarely truly overlap) and fails silently in production under real concurrency, giving you a plausible-looking but wrong diagnosis.
5

Traces and spans

Distributed tracing formalizes the correlation-ID idea into a standard shape.

Definitions: trace and span

A trace represents one complete business event's journey, e.g., one lead's full run through Receive → Validate → Enrich → Score → Assign, identified by a single trace ID shared across every step. A span represents one individual step within that trace, with its own start time, duration, and outcome. A trace is a collection of spans, all sharing the same trace ID, each span optionally aware of its parent span.

Why this matters for exactly the pipelines this course builds: Module 1's DAG is the natural shape of a trace, each node in that DAG is a candidate span, and the whole DAG run for one lead is a candidate trace. Observability isn't a separate concern bolted onto a workflow, in a well-designed system, the trace structure and the DAG structure are the same graph, viewed two different ways.
Practice check

Using the Module 1 pipeline (Receive → Validate → {Enrich, Score} → Assign → {Notify, Log}), how many spans would one complete trace for a single lead contain?

Check your answer
7, one span per node in the DAG: Receive, Validate, Enrich, Score, Assign, Notify, Log. Every node the lead passes through generates its own span, all sharing the trace ID for that specific lead's run.

Before the checkpoint: what you should now be able to do

A short consolidation before you're graded on it.

  • Define SLI, SLO, and error budget precisely, and state why an SLO without a time window is incomplete.
  • Compute allowed downtime from an SLO and a time window, and explain why each additional nine is harder than the last.
  • Explain the honest process for setting an SLI/SLO that reflects real user experience and real architectural capability.
  • List the minimum fields a structured log event needs, and explain why correlation ID is the most important one.
  • Define trace and span, and map a known DAG onto its corresponding trace structure.
Cross-module check

A retry storm (Module 3) is suspected of overwhelming a downstream API. What specific telemetry, from this module's toolkit, would let you confirm or rule that out, rather than just guessing from a vague "things feel slow" report?

Check your answer
Structured logs with a correlation ID and a retry-attempt-number field would let you filter to one failing trace and count how many attempts it actually made, and at what intervals, confirming whether backoff is spacing them out or whether they're bunching. Cross-referencing multiple traces' timestamps around the same window would reveal whether many different leads' retries are clustering together (the actual thundering-herd signature from Module 3, Section 1), rather than one lead just being slow. Without that data, "the CRM API seems slow" and "we're accidentally DDoS-ing our own dependency" are indistinguishable, exactly why Module 3's fix and Module 7's visibility have to go together in practice.

Glossary

SLI — Service Level Indicator; a specific numeric measurement of system behavior.
SLO — Service Level Objective; a target threshold for an SLI over a stated time window.
SLA — Service Level Agreement; a contractual consequence tied to missing an SLO.
error budget — 1 − SLO; the allowed amount of failure before the promise is broken.
structured log event — a machine-parseable log record with a fixed field set, including a correlation ID.
correlation ID / trace ID — an identifier shared by every log line belonging to one business event's full journey.
trace — one business event's complete journey across every step, made of spans sharing a trace ID.
span — one individual step within a trace, with its own duration and outcome.
