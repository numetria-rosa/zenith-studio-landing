Real-World Data Cleaning

This is where most analyst time actually goes. Nobody is going to hand you a list of what's wrong with a file, finding the problems is the skill. In this module you get raw data and an audit to perform, not a checklist to follow.

By the end of this module you'll be able to
  • Audit an unfamiliar dataset and find its problems without being told what they are
  • Distinguish a data-entry error from a real, surprising value
  • Fix duplicates, inconsistent categories, mixed currencies, and broken types
  • Decide between dropping, filling, and flagging, and justify the choice
  • Validate that your cleaning worked, instead of assuming it did
  • Document what you changed and why, so someone can audit your work
4.1

The discipline: never clean before you investigate

The instinct of a new analyst is to see something odd and immediately fix it. The instinct of a good analyst is to ask why it is there first. A negative quantity might be a typo, or it might be how this company records a return, and those two answers lead to opposite actions.

The six-step loop, used for every problem you find
Detect
Find the anomaly. Counts, uniques, ranges, and type checks surface almost everything.
Investigate
Look at the actual rows. How many? Are they clustered in one source, one date, one category?
Decide
Drop, fix, fill, or flag, and be able to say why in one sentence.
Clean
Apply the fix, ideally to a copy, never to your only version of the raw file.
Validate
Re-run the detection. Did the count go to zero? Did the row count change by the amount you expected?
Document
Write down what you changed and why. This is what makes your number defensible.
The rule that matters most: never silently delete data. Every row you drop is a decision you must be able to defend to someone who disagrees with you. "It looked wrong" is not a defence. "7 rows had negative quantities, all in the same week, none matched a return record, so I excluded them and flagged it to Ops" is.
4.2

Your detection toolkit

Six commands surface the overwhelming majority of real data problems. Learn these as a routine you run on every new file, before doing anything else.

CommandFinds
df.isna().sum()Missing values, per column
df.duplicated().sum()Exact duplicate rows
df["col"].duplicated().sum()A key column that should be unique but isn't
df["col"].unique()Inconsistent categories, casing, spelling variants
df.describe()Impossible values: negatives, absurd maximums, zero where zero is impossible
df.dtypesNumbers or dates that loaded as text
A tip that saves hours: df["col"].value_counts() is often better than .unique(), because it shows you how many of each value there are. A category variant appearing 200 times is a systemic issue; one appearing twice is probably a typo. The count changes what you should do about it.

4.4

Fixing what you found

Now the techniques, in the order you'd apply them. Each one is a tool; the judgement about whether to use it is the part that makes you an analyst.

Exact duplicates

df.drop_duplicates() removes rows identical across every column. Nearly always safe, a genuinely identical row is almost always a loading or export artifact. Compare row counts before and after so you know how many went.

Inconsistent text (casing, whitespace)

df["sku"] = df["sku"].str.strip().str.upper(). For categories, .str.title() is usually the right normalisation. Always check .nunique() before and after, a big drop tells you how much phantom variety you just collapsed.

Numbers trapped in text (currency symbols, thousands separators)

df["amount"].str.replace("$", "", regex=False).astype(float). Watch for European formatting, where , is the decimal separator, not a thousands separator, €95,46 is ninety-five euros, not nine thousand.

Mixed date formats

pd.to_datetime(df["order_date"], format="mixed", dayfirst=False) handles several formats in one column. Without format="mixed", newer pandas raises rather than guessing, which is the safer behaviour, because a wrong guess silently swaps days and months.

The most dangerous fix in this list: date parsing. 03/04/2026 is March 4th to an American system and April 3rd to a European one. There is no way to tell from the value alone. If the data source is ambiguous, you ask, you do not guess and hope.

4.5

Missing values: drop, fill, or flag

There is no universally correct answer. There is only a correct answer for this column, this analysis, and this business context, and a requirement that you can explain it.

Drop the rows

df.dropna(subset=["units"]). Reasonable when the missing rows are few and there's no reason to think they're systematically different. Dangerous when missingness correlates with something, if high-value orders are likelier to be missing a field, dropping them biases every number you produce afterwards.

Fill with a value

df["units"].fillna(0) or .fillna(df["units"].median()). Only when the filler is genuinely meaningful. Filling a missing price with 0 turns "we don't know" into "it was free," which will quietly wreck a revenue total.

Leave it and report it

Often the most honest option. pandas skips NaN in .sum() and .mean() anyway, so you can compute on what you have, as long as you state the count alongside the result. "Average order value was $47.20, computed across 393 of 400 orders; 7 lacked a valid quantity."

The distinction that separates blank from "MISSING": in the orders file, 133 blank shipped_date values are structural, those orders never shipped, so of course there's no date. That's not dirty data, it's information. The single "MISSING" string is different: a row that claims to have shipped but has no date, with a placeholder someone typed by hand. The first is expected and meaningful; the second is a genuine defect. Treating them the same would be a mistake in both directions.

4.6

Validation: prove the cleaning worked

Cleaning without validation is hoping. After every fix, re-run the detection that found the problem and confirm it's gone, and confirm you didn't break something else in the process.

# before
print("dupes:", df.duplicated().sum(), "rows:", len(df))
clean = df.drop_duplicates()
# after, both numbers should have moved by exactly the amount you expect
print("dupes:", clean.duplicated().sum(), "rows:", len(clean))
Validation questions worth asking every time

Did the row count change by exactly the number I intended?
Is the anomaly count now zero?
Did a total I care about move more than I expected? (a large swing means you removed more than you thought)
Do the categories now match the list the business actually uses?

ecommerce_orders.csv
