Data Visualization & Storytelling

Every chart here is drawn from numbers your own pandas code computes and Python actually verifies, not a static picture. The hard part was never the charting syntax, it's choosing the right chart for the question, and knowing when a chart is honest or when it's quietly lying.

By the end of this module you'll be able to
  • Match a question to the right chart family: distribution, comparison, or trend
  • Use a histogram to catch data-quality problems a table hides
  • Recognize when a rate calculated from a tiny sample is misleading, no matter how good it looks
  • Choose between a line and a bar chart based on which one actually reveals the pattern
  • Build the correct, honest chart for a real stakeholder question, and defend the choice
7.0

The three questions a chart can answer

Almost every chart is answering one of three questions. Get this wrong and no amount of polish saves the chart.

Distribution

"What do the values in this column look like, spread out?" Are they clustered, spread wide, skewed, or is there something that shouldn't be there at all? Histogram is the default tool.

Comparison

"How do these categories compare to each other, right now?" Product A vs B vs C, channel vs channel, stage vs stage. Bar chart is the default tool, ranked so the eye doesn't have to hunt.

Trend

"How did this number move over time?" Line chart is the default tool, but only when there's enough underlying signal to be worth connecting the dots, section 7.3 shows a case where it isn't.

Why not pie charts: the human eye is bad at comparing angles and areas, good at comparing bar lengths and dot positions. A bar chart almost always communicates the same comparison faster and more accurately than a pie chart. You won't find one in this module.

7.1

Distribution: what a histogram catches that a table hides

The dataset: survey_responses.csv, the same satisfaction survey from Module 5. satisfaction_score is meant to be a 1–5 rating.

Why bother charting it if you already computed mean/median in Module 5: a mean and a median are two numbers, they can't show you the shape. A histogram shows every value at once, including ones that don't belong.
Pattern

Convert the column to numeric, count how many rows fall into each distinct score value (don't drop anything yet), and look at the full shape, not just the valid range.

Exercise 1 · real data, real Python, graded

Write score_histogram(df): convert satisfaction_score to numeric, and return a dict mapping every distinct numeric score value present (as a string key, e.g. "0") to its row count. Do not filter anything out first, count every numeric value that appears, blanks excluded.

Comparison: the funnel as a bar chart

The dataset: app_analytics_events.csv, 11,635 real product-analytics events across one month, four stages: view_item, add_to_cart, start_checkout, purchase.

Business question: "Where in the funnel are we losing the most people?"

Pattern

Count events per stage, then look at the ratio from one stage to the next, not just the raw counts, a stage with a small count isn't automatically the problem if it started from a small pool.

7.3

Trend: when a line chart is the wrong chart

The dataset: the same event log, filtered to purchase events, across 30 days of May.

Business question: "Is there a weekly pattern in when people buy?"

The trap: a daily line chart of 30 noisy points invites you to eyeball a "trend" in what might just be day-to-day noise. The real pattern here isn't a trend over time at all, it's a repeating pattern by day of week, which a daily line chart is the wrong tool to reveal.
Pattern

Build both: a daily line (30 points, noisy) and a day-of-week bar chart (7 points, aggregated across all 30 days). Compare which one actually answers the question.

Exercise 3 · real data, real Python, graded

Write purchase_trends(df): return {"daily": {"2026-05-01": 46, ...}, "by_dow": {"Monday": ..., "Tuesday": ..., ..., "Sunday": ...}}, using each purchase's date and day-of-week name.

Which chart actually answers "is there a weekly pattern"?
7.4

The misleading chart: a rate from a tiny sample

The dataset: marketing_campaign_results.csv, the same 4-channel data from Module 5.

A chart someone on the marketing team already made

Referral looks like the best-performing channel by far. Before you recommend shifting budget to it, look at the sample it's built on.

The trap: Referral's conversion rate (9.52%) comes from 21 clicks and 2 conversions. One more or one fewer conversion swings that rate by roughly 5 points. Search's 4.79% comes from 1,815 clicks, that number barely moves if a handful of conversions go the other way. A bar chart with no sample size shown treats both numbers as equally trustworthy, they aren't.
Pattern (same "reliable" rule as Module 5)

A channel's rate is only comparable once it clears a minimum sample: at least 30 clicks and at least 5 conversions. Below that, report the number but don't rank it.

Exercise 4 · real data, real Python, graded

Write reliable_channel_rates(df): group by channel, compute each channel's conversion rate (conversions/clicks*100) and total clicks, then return only the channels meeting the reliable threshold above, as {"Search": {"cvr": 4.79, "clicks": 1815}, ...} (values rounded to 2 decimals).

No step-by-step here: you already know how to standardize a messy column (Module 4) and how to build a trend chart (7.3). This asks you to combine both on a shape you haven't practiced. Q1 = Jan–Mar, Q2 = Apr–Jun, Q3 = Jul–Sep, Q4 = Oct–Dec.
