8.0

What a dashboard actually is

A dashboard is not a chart collection. It's the end of a chain, and every link in that chain can break.

Business question→ Data→ KPI→ Analysis→ Visualization→ Dashboard→ Interpretation→ Decision
A tiny, made-up example, not the module's real data: a raw table of 5 lemonade-stand sales rows becomes a KPI ("$14 average sale"), becomes a chart (sales by day), becomes a dashboard (that chart plus a second one for weather), becomes a decision ("open Saturdays only, revenue per hour is triple"). Skip any one step, calculate the KPI wrong, or pick a chart that hides the pattern, and the decision at the end is built on nothing.

The failure mode this module is built around: a dashboard that looks completely professional, clean cards, a nice color palette, real interactive charts, and quietly answers the wrong question because a KPI was calculated on the wrong subset of rows. It will not look broken. It will look great. That's what makes it dangerous.
8.1

Start with the business question

Nobody asks for "a bar chart." They ask something like "how much are we actually selling, and is it working?" Your first job is never to open Plotly, it's to turn that into a small number of specific, calculable questions.

What is a KPI?

A Key Performance Indicator is one number, precisely defined, that a business tracks because it answers a real question. "Revenue" isn't precise on its own, revenue of what, over what period, counting which rows is what makes it a usable KPI.

Why would a business care?

Because a KPI is what a decision gets made on. If "average order value went up 40%" is wrong, the business might raise prices, cut a promotion, or congratulate a team, based on a number that was never real.

The scenario for the rest of this module: you're a junior analyst at an online store. Management wants to know how much you're actually selling, where the revenue is coming from, and whether it's growing. You have ecommerce_orders.csv, the same 412-row funnel export you cleaned in Module 4, four stages from cart_created to shipped.

8.2

Building KPIs in Python, and the trap hiding in the denominator

Not every row in ecommerce_orders.csv is a completed sale. status records how far the row got through the funnel: cart_created, checkout_started, payment, shipped. Only the last two mean the customer actually paid.

The trap: if you sum quantity × unit_price across every row regardless of status, you are counting abandoned carts and started-but-never-finished checkouts as if they were real revenue. It will produce a real-looking number. It will be wrong.
Exercise A · real Python, graded, required

Write store_kpis(df), returning a dict with total_revenue, total_orders, and average_order_value, counting completed sales only. df is already loaded and has a line_total column (quantity × unit_price) precomputed for you, the same cleaning from Module 4 (duplicates dropped, negative quantities removed) is already applied.

Hint 1, conceptual
Think about what one row in this dataset represents. Not every row is a sale, some are carts that were never paid for.
Hint 2, more specific
Look at the status column's four values. Which ones mean money actually changed hands?
Hint 3, technical
Filter with df[df["status"].isin(["payment", "shipped"])] before summing anything.

Choosing the right chart for a comparison, and the same trap in chart form

Business question: "which product category matters most to revenue?" This is a comparison across a small number of categories, so a bar chart is the right default, exactly like Module 7. The trap from 8.2 shows up here too, just harder to notice because a chart looks authoritative even when it's built on the wrong rows.

Exercise B · real Plotly, real Python, graded, required

Build a bar chart of total revenue by product_category, completed orders only. df is already loaded with line_total precomputed.

Hint 1, conceptual
Same filter as 8.2 applies here. A chart doesn't automatically know which rows count as real sales, you still have to tell it.
Hint 2, more specific
Filter to completed status first, then group by category and sum line_total.
Hint 3, technical
df[df["status"].isin(["payment","shipped"])].groupby("product_category")["line_total"].sum(), then reset the index and chart it with px.bar.
8.4

Trend charts, and the axis trick that lies without changing a single number

Business question: "is revenue actually growing?" The data spans April, May, and June 2026. Here's a chart someone on the team already built to answer that.

A chart someone already made · real code, run it yourself

Real, unedited code. Run it and look at what it shows.

The trap: June's revenue is about 17% below May's, a real dip worth investigating. But forcing the y-axis to start at 10,500 instead of 0 stretches that dip across almost the entire chart height, making it look like revenue is collapsing. Not one number in the underlying data changed. Only the axis did.
The fix

Let the y-axis include zero, or at minimum don't hand-pick a narrow range that exaggerates the visual swing. The real dip is still worth flagging, an honest chart lets the reader judge its actual size instead of being told how alarmed to feel.

8.5

Filters, without writing a filter

A real dashboard tool gives readers a dropdown to filter by category. You don't have a server here, but Plotly's legend already does most of that job for free: give a chart one trace per category with color=, and clicking a category in the legend hides or isolates it, live, in the browser, no extra code.

Exercise D · real Plotly, real Python, graded, required

Build a grouped bar chart of revenue by month, split by category (color="product_category"), completed orders only. Once it renders, click a category name in the legend, it will toggle in and out of the chart.

Hint 1, conceptual
This is 8.4's grouping, with one more dimension. Group by both month and category instead of month alone.
Hint 2, more specific
groupby(["month", "product_category"]) gives you one row per month/category pair.
Hint 3, technical
Pass color="category" and barmode="group" to px.bar, that's what creates one legend entry, and one clickable trace, per category.

8.8

The toolset, and where this course stops

You've now touched every tool a junior analyst actually uses. They overlap on purpose, and none of them is "the best one," each fits a different moment in the same chain from 8.0.

Excel

Fast, visible, zero setup. Best for quick exploration and small, one-off business workflows. Falls apart at scale and isn't repeatable without real discipline.

SQL

Retrieves and aggregates data that lives in a real database, often before Python ever sees it. The tool for "get me the right rows" at any scale.

Python / pandas

Deeper analysis, automation, and repeatability. The same cleaning and KPI logic runs identically every time, on data Excel would choke on.

Plotly

Interactive visualization for exploration and for embedding in an app or notebook. What you used all module.

BI tools (Tableau, Power BI, Looker)

Production dashboards meant for stakeholders who don't code, refreshed on a schedule, permissioned, shared company-wide. This course doesn't teach a specific one, the KPI-definition and chart-choice judgment you just practiced transfers directly.

Honest gap

This module builds one dashboard's worth of judgment. Production BI tools add scheduled refresh, row-level permissions, and drill-downs, genuinely useful next things to learn, not things this course claims to teach.

The analyst's actual job, in order

Stakeholder asks a vague question → you turn it into measurable questions → you define KPIs → you query and clean the data → you validate the calculation → you build the dashboard → you explain what it shows → the business decides. Building charts is one step out of seven. Most of the job is everything around it.

Want to rehearse the Tableau-specific reasoning? The Tableau Practice Library is a decision-based practice simulation, not real Tableau software, that drills Dimension vs Measure, discrete vs continuous, and which shelf a field belongs on, exactly the judgment this module built, applied to Tableau's specific vocabulary. The Power BI Practice Library is the same kind of honest simulation for Power BI's vocabulary, Column vs Measure, Calculated Column vs Measure, and Row Context vs Filter Context in DAX.
