5.1

Mean, median, and which one is lying to you

The mean is the average. The median is the middle value when you sort them. When the two disagree substantially, that disagreement is itself the finding.

The rule

Mean gets dragged by extreme values; median doesn't. If mean > median, something large is pulling the average up. If they're close, the data is roughly symmetric and either is fine.

The business version of this: "average order value is $180" sounds healthy. If the median is $40, what you actually have is a lot of small orders and a handful of enormous ones, a completely different business, needing a completely different strategy. Reporting only the mean would be technically true and practically misleading.
Never used these before? For [1, 2, 3, 4, 100]: the mean is 22 (add them, divide by 5), the median is 3 (the middle one). Notice the mean isn't near any actual value in the list. That's the whole lesson.

5.2

Standard deviation: the consistency question

Standard deviation measures spread, how far values typically sit from the mean. Low means tightly clustered and predictable; high means volatile.

When you'd actually reach for it: a stakeholder asks "which product line should we build the forecast around?" Two products both average $50k a month. One has a standard deviation of $3k, the other $28k. Same average, wildly different planning risk. The mean alone cannot answer their question; the standard deviation can.
How to explain it to a manager without saying "standard deviation"

"Product A does about $50k a month, give or take $3k. Product B also averages $50k, but it swings between $22k and $78k. If we're committing to inventory, A is the safer bet." You have just communicated a standard deviation without using the term. That translation is the actual skill.

5.3

The small-sample trap

Here is the most common way analysts get embarrassed in meetings. Look at these two marketing channels, both from the real campaign file.

Referral
9.52%
conversion rate
2 conversions from 21 clicks
Email
7.04%
conversion rate
20 conversions from 284 clicks

Referral looks like the best channel we have. It is 35% better than Email. An analyst who stops here recommends moving budget into Referral.

But look at the sample. Referral converted 2 people. If one of those two had not converted, the rate drops to 4.8%, worse than Email. A single person flipping changes the entire recommendation. That is not a finding; that is noise wearing a finding's clothes.
Confidence interval: the honest version of a number

A 95% confidence interval is the range the true value plausibly sits in, given how much data you have. Computed from the real numbers above:

Referral: −3.0% to 22.1%, so wide it includes negative conversion, which is impossible. That is the maths telling you it has no idea.
Email: 4.1% to 10.0%, a usable range you could plan against.

The plain-English rule: before comparing two rates, look at the denominators. If either is small (a rough rule of thumb: fewer than ~30 events), be very sceptical. Percentages computed from tiny numbers are unstable, and presenting them as findings is how analysts lose credibility.
5.4

Significance testing, and what a p-value is not

A significance test answers exactly one question: if there were genuinely no difference between these two groups, how surprising would data like this be? The p-value is that surprise, as a probability.

Reading a p-value

p = 0.671, data like this would be completely unsurprising even if there were no real difference. Conclude nothing.
p = 0.0002, data like this would be very surprising if there were no real difference. Something is probably going on.
The conventional cutoff is 0.05, but it's a convention, not a law of nature.

What a p-value does NOT mean (this trips up experienced people, not just beginners):
It is not the probability that your hypothesis is true.
It is not the probability the result was a fluke.
It says nothing about how big or important the difference is.
A tiny, commercially irrelevant difference will produce a very small p-value if your sample is large enough. Significance is about detectability, not importance.
Statistical vs. business significance: with 5 million users you can prove a 0.01% conversion lift is real (p < 0.001). That does not make it worth a six-month engineering project. Conversely a 5% lift on a small test might not reach significance yet still be worth investigating further. The test tells you whether the effect exists; only you and the business can say whether it matters.

5.5

Correlation, and the claim you must not make

Correlation measures whether two things move together, on a scale from −1 to +1. Zero means no linear relationship. It does not, ever, on its own, establish that one thing causes the other.

Three explanations for every correlation you find

1. A causes B, the one everyone jumps to.
2. B causes A, reverse causation. Busy stores schedule more staff, rather than more staff creating busyness.
3. C causes both, a confounder. Ice cream sales and drowning both rise in summer; neither causes the other.
A correlation alone cannot distinguish between these. Only an experiment, or strong domain knowledge, can.

The sentence that ends careers: "Customers who use feature X retain better, so we should push everyone to feature X." Maybe X causes retention. Or maybe already-engaged customers are the ones who find X. Push X on everyone and you may learn, expensively, that it was the second one.

Model answer (open after writing your own)
Recommendation: Scale Search, protect Email, hold Referral pending more data.

Evidence. Search delivers 87 conversions from 1,815 clicks (4.79%) at a $28.37 CPA. Its advantage over Social (2.37%, $51.93 CPA) is statistically significant at p = 0.0002, with samples this size, that difference is real, not noise. Social is our most expensive channel per acquisition and the weakest converter; it's the obvious source of reallocated budget.

Email converts best among channels with enough data to trust (7.04% on 284 clicks, $24.17 CPA, our cheapest acquisition). The constraint is volume, not performance. I'd grow list size rather than shift spend.

Referral shows 9.52%, the highest number on the page, and I want to be explicit that I am not recommending it on that basis. It rests on 2 conversions from 21 clicks. Its 95% interval runs from −3% to 22%, which is another way of saying we have no idea. One conversion either way swings it from best to below average. Recommendation: spend a small fixed test budget specifically to build sample size, and revisit in 4–6 weeks.

Limitations. Eight weeks of data, no seasonality adjustment, and no control for traffic quality differences between channels. CPA excludes fixed costs and creative production.
