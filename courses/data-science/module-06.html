6.1

SELECT, WHERE, ORDER BY, LIMIT

Business question: "Which 5 customers signed up most recently?"

Pattern

SELECT columns FROM table ORDER BY column DESC LIMIT n, pick the columns you want, sort by the one that defines "most recent," take the top few.

Exercise 1 · runs against the real database

Return each customer's name and signup_date, for the 5 most recently signed-up customers, most recent first.

6.2

Aggregates and GROUP BY

Business question: "Which product category sells the most units?"

Pattern

SELECT category, SUM(quantity) FROM ... JOIN ... GROUP BY category, GROUP BY collapses many rows into one row per category, SUM adds up the quantity within each group.

Why this needs a join: order_items has the quantity, products has the category, neither table has both, so you join them on sku first.
Exercise 2 · runs against the real database

Return category and total units (sum of quantity) sold in each category, across ALL order_items regardless of order status, highest first.

6.2b

CASE WHEN, and filtering a group with HAVING

Business question: "How many orders fall into each price tier: under $50, $50 to $200, or over $200?"

CASE WHEN: an if/else inside a query

SQL has no built-in "price tier" column, you build one inline: CASE WHEN unit_price < 50 THEN 'Low' WHEN unit_price < 200 THEN 'Mid' ELSE 'High' END AS tier. It reads top to bottom, like Python's if/elif/else, the first matching condition wins. You can GROUP BY the result of a CASE expression exactly like a real column.

HAVING: WHERE, but after grouping

WHERE filters individual rows before grouping happens. HAVING filters entire groups, after GROUP BY has collapsed them, usually to test an aggregate like HAVING COUNT(*) > 5 or HAVING SUM(revenue) > 1000. You cannot write WHERE SUM(revenue) > 1000, the aggregate doesn't exist yet at the point WHERE runs.

6.3

The NULL trap

Business question: "How many orders came through each channel?"

The trap: 11 orders have no recorded channel at all, the value is genuinely NULL, not the text "unknown." COUNT(channel) silently skips NULLs; COUNT(*) doesn't. A filter like WHERE channel != 'web' also silently excludes every NULL row, because SQL's three-valued logic treats NULL != anything as unknown, not true.
Pattern

GROUP BY channel puts NULL rows into their own group automatically, you'll see it appear as a group with no label. Use IS NULL / IS NOT NULL to test for it explicitly, never = or !=.

Exercise 3 · runs against the real database

Return channel and the count of orders (n) for each channel, including the NULL group, highest count first.

6.4

Joins, and the row-multiplication trap

Business question: "What's our total completed revenue by product category?"

The trap: most orders have more than one line item. Joining orders to order_items means each order can appear multiple times in the joined result, one row per item, not per order. If you COUNT(*) completed orders after this join, you'll get a bigger, wrong number (this database's real numbers: 77 completed orders, but 155 rows after joining to order_items). Revenue math is usually fine (you WANT one row per item to sum item revenue), but any count of orders after a join needs COUNT(DISTINCT order_id), not COUNT(*).
Pattern

Join order_items → orders → products, filter WHERE status = 'completed', then GROUP BY category summing quantity * unit_price.

Exercise 4 · runs against the real database

Return category and revenue (ROUND(SUM(quantity*unit_price),2)), counting only completed orders, highest revenue first.

Challenge (optional): prove the fan-out trap to yourself
Run SELECT COUNT(*) FROM orders o JOIN order_items oi ON oi.order_id=o.order_id WHERE o.status='completed' in the explorer above, then compare it to SELECT COUNT(*) FROM orders WHERE status='completed'. The gap between the two numbers is entirely orders with more than one line item.
6.5

Finding what ISN'T there

Business question: "Which customers have never placed an order?", a real re-engagement/win-back list.

Why this is harder than it sounds: there's no "never ordered" flag anywhere. You have to find customers who don't show up in orders at all, the absence of a match, not a value you can filter on directly.
Pattern

WHERE NOT EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM orders o WHERE o.customer_id = c.customer_id), for each customer, check whether any matching order exists at all. A LEFT JOIN + WHERE order_id IS NULL answers the same question a different way; either is correct.

Exercise 5 · runs against the real database

Return customer_id and name for every customer with zero orders, ordered by customer_id ascending.

6.6

Required exercise: combine everything

Business question, the kind a manager actually asks: "Which country's customers are worth the most to us, per customer?" Not raw country revenue, a big country wins that by default. Revenue per customer, so a small but valuable market doesn't get overlooked.

This needs, together

A join across all three of customers, orders, and order_items · a filter to completed orders only · GROUP BY country · COUNT(DISTINCT customer_id) so the fan-out trap from 6.4 doesn't inflate your customer count · and dividing total revenue by that customer count.

Required exercise · this module's graded checkpoint work

Return country, total_revenue, n_customers, and revenue_per_customer (all rounded to 2 decimals where relevant), for completed orders only, highest revenue_per_customer first.

The real finding: once you get this right, look at the result. The United Kingdom has the most customers and the most total revenue, and is NOT the top country per customer. That gap is a real, defensible insight, exactly the kind Module 8's dashboard work will ask you to explain to a stakeholder.
