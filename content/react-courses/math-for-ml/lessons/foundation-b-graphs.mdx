## Why this exists

Modules 4 and 5 ask you to read a slope, a gradient, and a rate of change
directly off a curve. This is the vocabulary for that - inputs, outputs,
and what "steepness" actually means on a graph.

## Inputs and outputs

A graph plots a function's inputs (usually along the horizontal axis) against
its outputs (vertical axis). Every point on the curve is one input paired
with the output that function produces for it.

## Slope: rate of change

<Callout tone="info" label="Definition">
**Slope** measures how much a line rises (or falls) for each unit it moves
right. A slope of 3 means: move 1 unit right, the line rises 3 units. A
slope of -2 means: move 1 unit right, the line falls 2 units.
</Callout>

<Callout tone="info" label="Worked example">
Find the slope of the line through the points $(1, 2)$ and $(4, 11)$:
1. Rise = change in output = $11 - 2 = 9$
2. Run = change in input = $4 - 1 = 3$
3. Slope = rise / run = $9 / 3 = 3$

So this line rises 3 units for every 1 unit it moves right - a slope of 3,
constant everywhere along a straight line.
</Callout>

<Callout tone="warning" label="Common mistake: dividing run by rise instead of rise by run">
Slope is $\frac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}}$ - change in output over change in
input - not the other way around. Flipping the fraction for the example
above would give $3/9 = 0.33$ instead of the correct $3$: a very different
number describing a very different steepness. When in doubt, remember
which axis is "output" (vertical, on top of the fraction) and which is
"input" (horizontal, on the bottom).
</Callout>

Straight lines have one constant slope everywhere. Curves don't - their
steepness changes from point to point, which is exactly the idea Module 4
turns into the derivative: the slope at one exact point, not averaged
over the whole curve.

## Reading a curve

A curve rising and getting steeper means its rate of change is itself
increasing. A curve flattening out means its rate of change is shrinking
toward zero. A perfectly flat point - a peak, a valley, or a plateau - has
a slope of exactly zero. All three of these readings come back directly
in Module 4 and 5's language around gradients and optimization.

<Callout tone="warning" label="Common mistake: confusing height with steepness">
A curve can be very *high up* on the graph while being perfectly *flat*
right at that point (think of the very top of a hill) - height and slope
are two separate readings. Conversely, a curve can cross through zero
height while still being very steep there. Always ask "how steep is it
here" and "how high up is it here" as two separate questions, since
Module 4's whole point is that the interesting quantity for optimization
is the steepness (the gradient), not the height (the loss value) alone.
</Callout>
