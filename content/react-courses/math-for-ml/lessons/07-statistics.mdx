## Why you're learning this

Module 6 showed that a single average settles down as you collect more
data. This module asks a sharper question: if you drew a *different* sample
of the same size, how different would that average likely have been? The
answer - the Central Limit Theorem - is why averages computed from
reasonably-sized samples can be trusted at all, and it's the mathematical
foundation under confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, and every
"statistically significant" claim you'll ever evaluate.

## 7.1 - Population vs. sample

<Callout tone="info" label="Definition">
A **population** is every possible item you could measure - every user,
every transaction, every possible experiment outcome. A **sample** is the
subset you actually collected data from. Almost everything in applied
statistics exists because you can only ever see a sample, but you want to
say something true about the whole population.
</Callout>

At n=1, each "sample mean" is just a single skewed value, so the histogram
looks like the population itself. At n=30, the histogram should look
noticeably more symmetric and bell-shaped, centered on the true population
mean - even though nothing about the underlying population changed.

## 7.4 - Where this shows up in real machine learning

- **Confidence intervals on model metrics** (accuracy, AUC) are built
  directly on the sampling-distribution idea: the reported number is a
  sample mean, and the interval reflects how much it could plausibly move
  if you re-measured on a different held-out set.
- **A/B testing** for product changes relies on the same math to decide
  whether an observed lift is larger than ordinary sampling variability
  would produce, or is likely just noise.
- **Correlation vs. causation** matters enormously in ML: a model can
  learn a real, useful correlation (a feature that predicts an outcome)
  without that feature causing the outcome - this distinction determines
  whether an intervention based on the model's insight will actually work.

## 7.5 - A failure mode this prevents

<Callout tone="warning" label="Common mistake: treating a small-sample result as settled fact">
A metric measured on a small holdout set (say, 30 examples) can look
dramatically different from the same metric measured on a much larger set,
purely from sampling variability - not because anything about the model
changed. Reporting "our new model is better" from a small, noisy comparison
without accounting for this is one of the most common and most avoidable
mistakes in applied ML evaluation.
</Callout>
