## Why you're learning this

Everything so far has described *what* things are - vectors, matrices,
directions that don't bend. This module introduces the mathematics of
*change*: how fast something is increasing or decreasing right now, at this
exact point. That single idea - the derivative, and its multivariable
cousin the gradient - is the entire mechanism behind how a machine learning
model learns anything at all. "Training a model" is, almost without
exception, "repeatedly asking a gradient which way is uphill, and stepping
the other way."

## 4.1 - A derivative is a slope, nothing more mysterious

If you zoom in far enough on any smooth curve, it starts to look like a
straight line. The slope of that line - how much the curve rises for a
tiny step to the right - is the derivative at that point. Positive slope
means the function is increasing there; negative means decreasing; exactly
zero means you're at a peak, a valley, or a flat spot.

<Callout tone="info" label="Definition">
The **derivative** of $f(x)$ at a point is the instantaneous rate of
change of $f$ there - how much $f$ changes per tiny unit of change in $x$.
For a function of two variables, a **partial derivative** does the same
thing but holds every other variable fixed while measuring the rate of
change in just one direction. The **gradient** bundles all of a
function's partial derivatives into a single vector.
</Callout>

<Callout tone="info" label="Worked example">
Compute $\nabla f$ by hand for $f(x, y) = x^2 + 3y^2$ at the point $(2, 1)$:
1. Partial with respect to $x$: treat $y$ as a constant, so $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2x = 2(2) = 4$
2. Partial with respect to $y$: treat $x$ as a constant, so $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 6y = 6(1) = 6$
3. Bundle them: $\nabla f = (4, 6)$

That arrow, $(4, 6)$, points toward steepest increase from $(2, 1)$ -
you can drag to that exact point in the lab below and check the reported
gradient matches.
</Callout>

## 4.4 - Where this shows up in real machine learning

- **Every optimizer** (plain gradient descent, SGD, Adam, all of them)
  works by computing a gradient and stepping opposite to it - the entire
  subject of Module 5.
- **Backpropagation** is the multivariable chain rule, applied
  systematically through every layer of a network, to compute the
  gradient of the loss with respect to every single weight.
- **Learning rate** controls how big a step to take along `−∇f` each time -
  too large, and you can overshoot the minimum; too small, and training
  crawls. You'll get to actually cause both failure modes in Module 5's
  lab.

## 4.5 - A failure mode this prevents

<Callout tone="warning" label="Common mistake: gradient descent means following the gradient">
The name is misleading if you don't know the math: gradient *descent*
follows the <b>negative</b> gradient, not the gradient itself. This is a
genuinely common source of sign errors when implementing optimization by
hand - a single missing minus sign turns an optimizer that decreases loss
into one that actively increases it, and the resulting "training curve
going the wrong way" bug can be surprisingly hard to spot if you don't
know exactly what to check first.
</Callout>

<Callout tone="warning" label="Common mistake: stopping at a zero PARTIAL derivative instead of a zero GRADIENT">
Finding one partial derivative equal to zero (say, $\partial f/\partial x = 0$)
does not mean you're at a minimum - the OTHER partial derivative might
still be nonzero, meaning the surface is still sloped in that direction.
A genuine flat point requires every single component of $\nabla f$ to be
zero simultaneously. Checking only one direction and declaring victory is
a common shortcut that silently misses saddle points and still-sloped
regions - exactly the kind of point an optimizer would keep moving from,
correctly, because it isn't actually flat yet.
</Callout>
