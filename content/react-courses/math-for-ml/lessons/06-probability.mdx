## Why you're learning this

Machine learning isn't just pattern recognition — a huge part of it is
reasoning honestly about uncertainty. A model's "87% confidence" is a
probability statement, and probability statements only mean something once
you understand what they promise (and, just as important, what they
don't). This module builds that foundation from the simplest possible
example: a biased coin.

## 6.1 — One flip tells you almost nothing

Flip a coin once and you learn almost nothing about its true bias — heads
or tails, either outcome is compatible with almost any underlying
probability. Flip it a thousand times, and the fraction of heads has to
converge toward the coin's real probability, because lucky and unlucky
stretches statistically cancel out over a long enough run.

<Callout tone="info" label="Definition">
**Expectation** is the long-run average outcome of a random process.
**Variance** measures how spread out individual outcomes are around that
average. The **law of large numbers** says the average of many independent
trials converges toward the true expectation as the number of trials
grows — not because any individual trial "corrects" for previous ones, but
because randomness averages out over a large enough sample.
</Callout>

## 6.4 — Where this shows up in real machine learning

- **Model confidence scores** are themselves probabilities, and are only
  meaningfully calibrated in aggregate, across many predictions — not as a
  guarantee about any one specific case.
- **Evaluation metrics computed on a test set** (accuracy, precision,
  recall) are themselves estimates with their own standard error — a
  metric measured on 50 examples is far noisier than the same metric
  measured on 50,000.
- **A/B testing and experiment design** in ML products rely directly on
  this module's math to decide how much data is actually needed before a
  measured difference can be trusted as real rather than noise.

## 6.5 — A failure mode this prevents

<Callout tone="warning" label="Common mistake: trusting a metric from too little data">
A validation accuracy of "92%" measured on only 40 examples carries a much
larger standard error than the same 92% measured on 4,000 examples — but
both often get reported with the same confident-sounding single number.
Before trusting a reported metric, this module's math gives you a concrete
way to ask "how much data was that computed from, and how much would that
number plausibly move if I collected more?"
</Callout>
